Abstract.
Blockchain oracles connect smart contracts to external information, but their records use
different units and accounting conventions. We present a data resource covering eight oracle branches,
with ﬁve detailed panels and three narrower extensions. Native observations are linked to protocol rules
and reconstructed lifecycles, distinguishing conﬁgured incentives, accruals, entitlements, payments and
applied losses. UTC annotations preserve event times, epoch intervals and snapshots separately. Two
derived products provide daily measurements within documented coverage and a request-level UMA pre-
diction task with proposal-time inputs and protocol-resolution labels. Validation examines native iden-
tities, economic classiﬁcations, settlement accounting and a cross-chain dispute. The resulting resource
supports descriptive research on participation, rewards and accountability while retaining differences in
mechanism, asset and observation unit. Missing evidence remains explicit, and protocol adjudication is
distinguished from independent veriﬁcation of external facts.
1 Introduction
Blockchain oracles make a fundamental institutional choice visible: a programmable contract must rely on
someone, or some mechanism, to supply information that the ledger itself cannot observe. Prices, event
outcomes and other external facts become inputs to deterministic execution through systems of reporting,
veriﬁcation and dispute resolution [1, 2]. The resulting arrangements combine technical infrastructure with
economic commitments. Participants may earn rewards, post capital, challenge a report or incur a penalty,
while users depend on the process that determines which information the contract accepts. Studying this
infrastructure therefore requires attention to both information provision and the incentives that sustain it [3,
4].
Public visibility of transactions does not, by itself, make these incentives measurable. The record of a
single transfer may combine returned principal with a reward, and a negative accounting adjustment may
precede any applied reduction in a participant’s stake. A disputed request can traverse several contracts
or chains before reaching settlement. Differences in protocol versions, native identiﬁers and accounting
conventions further complicate comparison. These are substantive measurement problems: an analysis that
treats gross payouts as proﬁt, or accruals as realized losses, changes the economic object being studied. A
useful comparative dataset must reconstruct the relevant lifecycle while retaining the institutional meaning
of each observed state.
Oracle arrangements expose different routes from information provision to economic accountability.
Some provide records of proposals, challenges and adjudication, whereas others expose staking operations,
reward epochs, signed updates or withdrawals. These differences motivate a resource organized around
observable mechanisms and historical implementations. Accordingly, the scope of Oracle4SD Atlas reﬂects
both substantive variation across mechanisms and the evidence retained for reconstruction. The resulting
branches support comparisons among speciﬁed activities; they do not constitute a census of oracle services
or a probability sample of the oracle ecosystem.
We address this problem with a layered data resource covering eight oracle branches. UMA, Chainlink,

2 Related work
Research on oracles and research on blockchain datasets approach the same infrastructure from complemen-
tary directions. Oracle studies examine how external information is authenticated, aggregated and incen-
tivized; data-resource studies establish reusable representations of transactions, entities and contracts. The
present work connects these perspectives by making the relationship between native protocol evidence and
economic measurement explicit. Tables 1 and 2 summarize representative contributions without treating
their differing units or purposes as directly comparable measures of scale.

2.1 Oracle design, security and economic institutions
Town Crier uses trusted hardware to authenticate external data delivery, whereas DECO develops crypto-
graphic proofs about data obtained over TLS [1, 5]. The oracle systematization of knowledge situates such
designs within a wider set of trust assumptions, attacks and mitigations [2]. Subsequent work addresses
other parts of the information-security problem: adaptive conformal consensus provides guarantees under
speciﬁed conditions for aggregating oracle reports, and SMARTCAT detects contracts associated with price-
manipulation attacks [6, 7]. These contributions clarify how oracle information can be delivered or threat-
ened. Their stated objectives differ from reconstructing the participant-level sequence between an incentive
commitment and its eventual economic realization.
Empirical oracle studies provide a second foundation. A ﬁrst look at DeFi oracles measures deployed
price-oracle behavior, while Demystifying Pythia examines Chainlink usage, pricing and service quality on
Ethereum [8, 9]. Economic research complements these observations by analyzing incentives, staking, de-
centralization and the integration of oracle networks with ﬁnancial markets [4, 10]. Together, these studies
motivate an empirical representation that follows the economic process across participation, challenge, ad-
judication and settlement. The framework developed here retains those stages where the source evidence
supports them, rather than assuming that every mechanism exposes an equivalent lifecycle.
The mechanisms represented in the Atlas expose different empirical objects. UMA and Tellor provide set-
tings for studying contestable reporting, challenge participation and adjudicated outcomes [22, 24]. Chain-
link’s staking and feed records distinguish economic participation from observable service updates [23],
while Flare FTSOv2 separates reward entitlements, provider conditions and subsequent claims [25]. Pyth’s
OIS records connect publisher-related incentive states with staking operations and economic transfers within
the retained historical window [26]. The narrower Chronicle, RedStone Push and DIA extensions add ob-
servations of challenge mechanisms, signed update delivery and staking withdrawals, respectively [27–29].
These branches contribute different parts of the measurement problem; their native records are not inter-
changeable observations of a universal oracle process.
Oracle economics and empirical studies of deployed services motivate questions about incentives, mon-
itoring, participation and service provision [4, 8–10]. Addressing these questions requires identifying the
observation unit, applicable rule and economic stage represented by each record. The Atlas contributes
reusable observations and reconstruction materials for this purpose. Protocol documentation provides ev-
idence about intended mechanisms, whereas historical execution records support claims about observed
behavior. Neither a valid signature, agreement with a protocol’s consensus outcome nor a recorded payment
independently establishes the correctness of the underlying external information. Connections to mechanism
design are research motivations, not claims that a deployed protocol satisﬁes a theoretical equilibrium or
security theorem.
2.2 Reusable blockchain data resources
Graph datasets make blockchain activity accessible to a broader research community. Elliptic represents
Bitcoin transactions for illicit-activity classiﬁcation; Elliptic2 shifts attention to labeled subgraphs of en-
tity clusters [11, 12]. Chartalist provides machine-learning-ready datasets for UTXO- and account-based
blockchains, while temporal Bitcoin datasets support entity and transaction graph analysis [13–15]. EX-
Graph connects Ethereum and X, and the multi-chain Graphs of Graphs resource preserves relationships
within and across token networks [16, 17]. These representations capture important network structure. Ora-
cle economics additionally requires identifying what a request, report, entitlement or applied balance change
means within its originating mechanism.
Other datasets broaden the range of relevant units. Research on NFTs and memecoins links market
behavior to network and visual features [18, 19]; event-aware DeFi forecasting emphasizes ﬁne-grained on-
chain events [20]. DIVE organizes smart contracts into separate pre- and post-deployment representations
for vulnerability analysis [21]. The latter illustrates the value of preserving multiple views of one underlying
object, a principle also used here. Our economic components, however, describe oracle participation and
ﬁnancial accountability rather than contract vulnerabilities, and their annotations depend on protocol-speciﬁc

The unresolved measurement need is thus not simply a larger transaction collection. Comparative ora-
cle research needs shared concepts that remain connected to native roles, timing and economic states. This
resource supplies that connection through layered representations, documented reconstruction rules and ex-
plicitly scoped validation. It complements the existing literature by distinguishing an announced incentive
from a claimable entitlement, a transfer from participant gain, and an institutional decision from external
truth. Those distinctions permit comparison without erasing the differences that the comparison seeks to
understand.

3 Methods
Construction proceeds from protocol evidence to economic interpretation, and only then to common analyti-
cal ﬁelds. This order matters because raw blockchain events do not deﬁne interchangeable economic observa-
tions. The workﬂow in Figure 2 ﬁrst registers historical sources and decodes native records, then reconstructs
supported lifecycles and economic states, and ﬁnally harmonizes the resulting observations. Unresolved iden-
tities, open lifecycles and unobservable outcomes remain explicit throughout. The method therefore treats
a common schema as a structured comparison of evidence, rather than a substitute for protocol-speciﬁc
reconstruction.
3.1 Scope, selection and historical boundaries
The discovery registry contains 56 oracle or related mechanism categories and 366 recorded protocol–oracle
relationships. It supports source discovery and ecosystem description, rather than deﬁning a probability
sampling frame. Within this broader registry, the present release contains eight oracle branches. Selection re-
ﬂects the economic or accountability process exposed by each implementation, the availability of identiﬁable
historical records and the extent to which those records support interpretable reconstruction. Here, a branch
denotes a speciﬁed protocol mechanism and implementation scope, together with the chain roles represented
in its observations.
UMA, Chainlink, Tellor, Flare FTSOv2 and Pyth provide ﬁve detailed native panels. Chronicle, RedStone
Push and DIA provide three narrower extensions covering selected challenge, update-delivery or staking ac-
tivities. This distinction describes the breadth of reconstruction in this release; it does not imply complete his-
torical coverage, identical variable availability or a ranking of protocol quality. Other versions, deployments
and services remain outside the release unless explicitly included in the source register. Table 3 summarizes
each branch’s measurement role and scope; Appendix A records implementation identiﬁers, source bindings
and exclusions.
Chain roles are distinguished by function. A veriﬁcation or adjudication chain records the relevant accep-
tance or dispute-resolution process; an economic settlement chain records the associated payment or applied
balance change; and a delivery chain records provision of the oracle output to a consuming application. A
single network may serve several roles. In the linked UMA disputes studied here, Polygon hosts the OOv2
lifecycle and observed local settlement ﬂows, whereas Ethereum hosts VotingV2 adjudication. Linking these
records establishes a relationship between protocol objects and does not, by itself, establish a cross-chain to-
ken transfer. The eight branches consequently do not denote eight independent blockchains.
The common upper observation cutoff is 30 June 2026 at 23:59:59 UTC. Table-level temporal support is
documented separately from protocol history in Appendix B. The temporal register identiﬁes deployment-
bound scans, implementation changes, retained-history limits and cohort restrictions without treating a ﬁrst
event as a deployment date. Lower boundaries vary with deployment, implementation changes, historical
source availability and collection limits. Project founding dates, production-service launches, deployment
boundaries, scanned intervals and ﬁrst observed events are distinct quantities. An interval between the ﬁrst
and last retained events does not establish uninterrupted observation. Transaction histories and retained
state panels may also have different temporal support, as in the Pyth OIS branch. Historical interpretation
requires an implementation applicable to the observation; unresolved source bindings and acquisition gaps re-
main limitations. Current protocol documentation supplies architectural context [22–29], while Appendix A
distinguishes that context from retained historical evidence.

interval and retrieval interface. EVM-compatible branches use historical logs, supplemented where necessary
by transactions, receipts, block metadata and token-transfer evidence. ERC-20 transfers are retained when
they help separate principal, fees and rewards from a settlement event. Tellor instead combines tellor-1
module state, ABCI events and block results; multiple reports within one block produce a one-to-many
expansion into 82,866,640 report observations. Pyth evidence is located by Solana transaction signatures

Source qualiﬁcation applies the registered version, cutoff and deterministic decoding conditions. For
Polygon UMA, 626,578 raw records include ﬁve unknown event topics that remain in the raw collection
but are excluded from the recognized decoded event families. For Chainlink staking, 175,015 collected

outcomes; they are not recoded as absent economic behavior. Native tables retain the strongest available
source keys and monetary amounts in integer units of the underlying asset, with asset identiﬁers and decimal
precision recorded separately. This preserves both the identity of an observation and the arithmetic needed
to interpret it.
3.3 Lifecycle reconstruction and cross-chain linkage
Decoded records are joined where the native mechanism identiﬁes a relationship between successive states.
UMA requests link proposals, disputes, DVM adjudication and settlement through reconstructed request
identiﬁers. For requester r, identiﬁer i, timestamp t and ancillary data a, the identiﬁer is
q = Keccak256(pack(r,i,t,a)),
(1)
where pack denotes the protocol’s packed ABI encoding. The reconstructed OOV2 panel contains 42,676
request rounds: 41,826 settle by the cutoff and 850 remain open. Other mechanisms require different objects.
For example, 46,723 retained Chainlink ETH/USD answer updates deﬁne 46,722 adjacent service windows,

and 13 Tellor jail events are paired with 13 release events to reconstruct reporter restrictions.
Cross-chain identity is established through version-aware ancillary-data transformations and native re-
quest construction. A UMA link receives Grade A only when the registered bridge or adapter rule identiﬁes
one unique DVM request. Of 835 attempted links, 832 meet this deterministic deﬁnition and three remain un-
matched. The grade describes the identity of a reconstructed object; it does not certify the factual correctness
of the oracle’s decision. Retaining unmatched and unﬁnished objects prevents the apparent completeness
of an analytical table from replacing evidence about the underlying process. The worked case links request,
proposal, dispute, DVM resolution and Polygon settlement. Each endpoint is located by its chain, transaction
and log position. The retained evidence supports the object linkage and elapsed intervals; intermediate relay
operations are represented separately where their transactions are not retained.
3.4 Economic states and settlement accounting
An economic observation is classiﬁed by its state and evidentiary basis. Table 4 distinguishes conﬁgured
incentives, accrued rewards or penalties, claimable entitlements, realized payments, applied losses, principal,
refunds and fees. The distinctions are protocol-speciﬁc. UMA challenge status requires native challenge
evidence, and settlement is not inferred merely from a transfer-like event. Tellor SUPPORT outcomes refer
to acceptance of the challenge and rejection of the challenged report. Negative DVM accruals remain distinct
from an observed reduction in staked principal. Pyth reward rates are not token reward amounts, and Flare
participation restrictions are not automatically monetary losses.
UMA settlement provides a concrete accounting example. Let B be the effective bond, F the ﬁnal fee
and R the conﬁgured reward, all measured in the smallest integer unit of the same asset. Posted principal is
P = B+F. For disputed requests, the identiﬁable winner component is W = B−⌊B/2⌋, while the protocol
component is Fprotocol = ⌊B/2⌋+F. Expected gross payouts follow the registered contract and adapter rules:
Gundisputed = B+F +R,
Gdisputed = B+F +W +Rsettlement.
(2)
Here Rsettlement denotes the reward component attributable to the settlement under the applicable adapter; a
refund or rollover is not automatically participant income. Reconciliation uses exact integer arithmetic and
checks ∆payout = Gactual −Gexpected against zero.
In the worked settlement described in Section 6.6, expressed in human-readable token units, a gross
payout of 1,000 comprises 750 units of returned principal and 250 units of token gain before the additional
costs needed to calculate net proﬁt. A separately conﬁgured reward of ﬁve units follows its observed refund or
rollover treatment. This example illustrates why transfer, principal recovery, token gain and net proﬁt require
separate measures. Funds-ﬂow checks apply the same logic where evidence permits: UMA settlements are
compared with token movements, Chainlink distinguishes transfer-requiring events from accounting-only
adjustments, and Flare claim reconciliation does not invent components that an aggregate claim fails to
expose.
3.5 Harmonization, missingness and documentation
Supported native concepts are projected into common ﬁelds for protocol, unit, role, actor or beneﬁciary,
lifecycle identity, time, asset, economic stage, source and evidence status. The common accountability layer
contains 105,588,120 typed observations. The economic-semantic layer contains 22,723,389 components,
and its stricter realized representation contains 19,275,854 components that satisfy branch-speciﬁc payment
or applied-state criteria. These layers overlap. Their purpose is to offer complementary views of the same
processes, not three independent populations.
Timestamp availability is also distinct from observation coverage. A valid clock on every retained row
does not establish that every eligible event was collected. Conversely, a missing common-layer clock does
not imply that the source event was absent. Epoch-only and snapshot-only records retain their appropriate
clocks while their canonical event time remains null. Missingness is interpreted as part of the measurement.
An absent value may indicate an inapplicable mechanism, an unexposed quantity, incomplete collection,
a decoding problem or an unﬁnished lifecycle; it is not automatically an observed zero. Where supported,

3.6 Temporal harmonization
Native timestamps are expressed in UTC while retaining their original values and precision. Each time ﬁeld is
documented by its source, unit and role in the protocol. The ﬁeld event_time_utc identiﬁes when an event
was recorded or executed. Request-reference timestamps, oracle payload times and scheduled deadlines
are represented separately because they can refer to a different point in the lifecycle. Records sharing a
timestamp retain their native block, transaction, log or instruction order.
Unix-second and millisecond values are converted without rounding. Textual timestamps are interpreted
using their recorded timezone; timezone-naive values are treated as UTC where this convention is established
by the source. Missing EVM event times are recovered only when another retained record identiﬁes the times-
tamp of the same block on the same chain. Conﬂicting matches are rejected and unmatched observations
remain undated. The provenance of recovered timestamps is recorded with the data.
Epoch observations retain their start and end times as half-open intervals [ae,be). Payment, snapshot and
retrieval times describe separate events and are stored independently. In particular, an epoch-end label does
not establish when a reward became claimable or was paid. Historical state recovered from a later snapshot
also requires care when assessing information available at a past decision time.
Daily windows begin at midnight UTC, and weekly windows run from Monday to the following Monday.
Epoch totals remain attached to their native support intervals. Daily aggregation therefore requires an event-
level measure or an explicit allocation rule, together with evidence that the relevant period was observed.

3.7 UMA prediction-task construction
The prediction task asks whether UMA’s eventual resolution differs from the proposed price. Each observa-
tion is one request, and the decision time T0 is the proposal event. For proposed price pi and resolved price
zi, the label is Yi = 1{zi ̸= pi}. Native values are compared as integers. Indeterminate and early-expiration
values retain their own classes; neither is recoded as a binary answer.
The cohort comprises challenged requests with a unique deterministic DVM link, consistent resolution
values and a reconciled settlement transfer. These conditions are evaluated retrospectively. The task therefore
concerns adjudication among selected disputes, rather than the probability that an arbitrary new request will
be challenged. Challenge, resolution and settlement ﬁelds are retained as outcome evidence and excluded
from the feature set.
The sixteen candidate features describe incentive amounts, timing, ancillary-data length, participant his-
tory and adapter identity. Participant histories are calculated separately for proposers and requesters, using
only requests that settled before the current proposal in native execution order. Rates are missing when no
prior settlement is retained. Proposal latency begins at the request event, while liveness ends at the proposal’s
expiry. Parameter-derived features carry a separate availability status because a value reconstructed from a
historical adapter requires evidence that the relevant parameter was available at T0. Appendix F gives the
complete deﬁnitions and information boundary.
3.8 Daily economic measurements
Daily observations group supported events within midnight-to-midnight UTC intervals. Each measure re-
tains its branch, implementation, actor role, asset, observation unit and economic stage. The feasibility
assessment distinguishes derivable quantities from those requiring additional acquisition, quantities inappli-
cable to the sampled mechanism, and unresolved cases. Appendix D describes the aggregation rules and
their denominators.
Payments enter the daily product only under the relevant realization rule. UMA rewards are separated
10

from returned principal and forfeited bonds; Tellor withdrawals into stake and Pyth transfers to stake custody
retain those destinations. Flare fee burns are excluded from participant payments. Chainlink claims require
a matched transfer and an execution timestamp. An unobserved day is left unﬁlled, and totals over retained
records are not presented as complete protocol totals.
Participation measures count addresses within a named role. Reward concentration is calculated among
recipients with positive observed payments, separately by asset and economic stage. For completed UMA
requests, delay runs from the request event to settlement, and committed-capital exposure is allocated across
days by the duration of each funded position. These completed-position measures do not represent unre-
solved capital obligations. Flare signing weights and thresholds are retained on their native epoch intervals;
they are not interpreted as daily payments or token collateral.

4.2 Common accountability and economic representations
The common accountability table contains 105,588,120 rows and 50 columns. Each row is a typed projection
of a native observation, retaining protocol identity, unit, roles, source references and evidence status. The
population includes requests, individual reports, disputes, epochs, staking observations and service windows.
Protocol, native table, unit type and unit identity must consequently accompany any cross-protocol aggrega-
tion. The representation provides shared analytical vocabulary while preserving the information needed to
determine whether two rows are comparable.
Economic components are represented in a separate 22,723,389-row, 28-column table. Its ﬁelds record
the mechanism, stage, actor, counterparty, amount, asset, source object, evidence class and validation status.
A stricter 19,275,854-row representation uses the same physical schema but requires qualifying evidence of
payment or an applied economic change. It overlaps the broader component table and is not an independent
sample. Stage, actor, asset and the documented grouping of related components must be considered together:
adding an accrued penalty to a later applied slash can count the same economic process twice.

entries identify the episode, decision time, challenge deadline, cross-chain link grade and provenance. Their
deﬁnitions specify units, derivation, decision-time availability, evidence class, missingness and validation
rules. Separately, the physical catalogue describes 1,789 ﬁeld entries across 95 Parquet tables. A conceptual

4.4 Temporal metadata
Temporal annotations form a separate data layer linked to the native records. They retain the original times-
tamps and identiﬁers and add UTC ﬁelds for the clocks supported by each source. Their deﬁnitions specify
the observation unit, temporal meaning, precision and treatment of missing values. Because the annotations
and the original common representation are separately versioned, analyses should identify which temporal
representation they use.
The temporal metadata describe the available interval and missing timestamps for each table. They also
distinguish execution times, epoch support, scheduled deadlines and later snapshots. This information allows
users to select comparable observation periods without treating all timestamped records as instantaneous
events.
4.5 Derived products and supporting evidence
The UMA task contains one row per eligible request, a sixteen-ﬁeld feature dictionary and a feature-level
availability record. The daily product contains typed measurements, recipient-level payment aggregates,
completion delays and committed-capital exposure, together with a separate historical Flare signing-policy
table. The latter retains epoch support because signing weights are deﬁned by the policy interval.
The correction ledger records old and revised ﬁeld values for identiﬁable native objects. The cross-chain
case retains the event register, elapsed intervals, cashﬂow components and unresolved evidence links. These
supporting tables document how the measurements were obtained; their rows are not additional independent
oracle observations. Appendix C describes the ten products and their relationships.

6 Technical validation
Validation assesses whether speciﬁed reconstructions satisfy structural, economic and computational require-
ments. Its strength depends on the tested population and acceptance rule. File readability, a unique identiﬁer,
a balanced settlement identity and successful replay establish different properties; none alone demonstrates
complete historical acquisition or independent oracle truth. We therefore distinguish the release’s docu-
mented checks, direct checks on retained tables, historical protocol-level validation records and bounded
computational replay.

resent different observation units. Cross-chain checks additionally assess whether the linked records identify
the same protocol object.

6.3 Computational reproducibility
Reconstruction was evaluated using archived inputs and a worked UMA case. The case links native events
to a request lifecycle and reconciles the resulting economic records with the associated token movements.
Comparisons of repeated archived executions assess whether the same inputs and code produce consistent
scientiﬁc outputs.
These evaluations concern reconstruction from retained evidence. Reacquiring historical data also de-
pends on archive access, interface behaviour and the original collection boundaries. The acquisition records
document those conditions separately from the computational checks.
6.4 Temporal consistency
Temporal validation compares converted timestamps with the native values and checks that the original obser-
vations and their ordering are preserved. Millisecond timestamps retain their fractional-second components
16

after conversion. Record selection over the tested daily and weekly windows agrees between the native and
UTC representations. Epoch boundaries are checked against the retained start and end ﬁelds, including the
assignment of observations at interval boundaries.
Missing timestamps are assessed separately from event absence. Exact block matching improves temporal
availability for some EVM records, while unresolved observations retain a missing-time status. Appendix B
summarizes these limitations and the distinction between timestamp availability and historical coverage.
6.5 Daily aggregation and prediction-task checks
Daily validation checks that grouped event counts and integer monetary totals agree with their eligible native
inputs. Capital exposure is checked by comparing the sum of daily interval overlaps with the corresponding
position-level duration. A valid timestamp is required for assignment to a day; eligible observations without
one remain in the source population and in the missing-time accounting.
The prediction-task checks examine request uniqueness, cohort membership, target construction and fea-
ture timing. Role-history counts and rates are compared with a separate direct selection of prior settlements.
The checks also preserve the distinction between a native event observed by the proposal and a historical
parameter whose availability requires additional source binding. Feature distributions and missingness are
provided in Appendix F to support inspection of the task data.
6.6 Worked cross-chain settlement
The worked UMA dispute connects four Polygon transactions with one Ethereum resolution.
Polygon
records the request, proposal, challenge and local settlement; Ethereum records the VotingV2 decision. Re-
ceipt status, block identity, event position and the linked request identiﬁer are checked against the retained
evidence. Figure 5 shows the elapsed timeline and the settlement decomposition; Appendix G provides the
event register and unresolved relay boundaries.
The request is proposed after about 7.7 hours and challenged shortly afterwards. Approximately 92.2
hours separate the challenge from DVM resolution, followed by about 22 minutes to Polygon settlement.
The longer interval includes transport, scheduling and adjudication, which cannot be separated using the
ﬁve endpoints alone. The resolved value is the protocol’s early-expiration value, distinct from either binary
answer.
Each disputing party posts 750 USDC.e. The winner receives 1,000 USDC.e, comprising 750 in returned
principal and 250 in gain; the protocol fee recipient receives 500. The separately conﬁgured reward follows
its observed refund or rollover treatment. The recorded participant and fee ﬂows balance, but transaction
costs and other costs would be needed to calculate net proﬁt. The identity link between chains does not
establish that these funds crossed a bridge.
7 Research use and interpretation
Research use should begin with a mechanism-speciﬁc question and an explicitly deﬁned observation unit.
Table 10 connects the eight branches to research questions, available observations and additional evidence
requirements. These mappings identify opportunities for reuse rather than report tests of the proposed hy-
potheses. Analyses should retain implementation version, actor role, asset, economic stage and observation
window, and establish comparability for the particular measure being studied. A shared schema facilitates
this work but does not, by itself, identify causal effects or make different native activities interchangeable.
Parquet tables can be read with DuckDB, PyArrow or compatible analytical software. Column selection
and predicate ﬁltering allow work on the large tables without loading every row into memory; the repository
notebook demonstrates a smaller reconstruction route. The catalogue documents ﬁle locations and relation-
ships, while the dictionaries deﬁne the variables and their interpretation. Monetary values require their asset
and decimal precision, timestamps require the branch’s native chronology, and physical row order is not an
event-time deﬁnition. Addresses represent protocol actors or accounts rather than independently identiﬁed
natural persons.

A Scope and sources
A.1 Selection and scope boundaries
This appendix documents the eight branches at the level of the sampled mechanism and implementation.
Selection combines a measurement contribution with identiﬁable historical evidence: contestable reporting
and adjudication for UMA and Tellor; staking and selected feed service for Chainlink; entitlement and
claim stages for Flare FTSOv2; publisher–epoch incentives and staking operations for Pyth; and narrower
challenge, Push-update and withdrawal observations for Chronicle, RedStone and DIA. Table 3 identiﬁes the
corresponding boundaries. The registry is a discovery resource, not a probability sample or an exhaustive
census. Exclusions describe this release and do not establish that an economic activity or service is absent
elsewhere in a protocol.
Public events alone are insufﬁcient for inclusion in an economic comparison. An observation must have an
interpretable native unit and an identiﬁable implementation and source. Economic amounts additionally re-
quire an asset and a defensible accounting stage. This explains why a signed update can enter a delivery panel
without supplying a publisher-payment observation, and why a withdrawal can support principal/reward de-
composition without identifying the correctness of a price report. The distinctions also constrain the research
opportunities in Table 10.

provide a possible time reference. This lookup uses both identiﬁers and accepts only a consistent timestamp.

D Daily measurement deﬁnitions
D.1 Feasibility and coverage
Feasibility is assessed for each branch and measurement family before aggregation. A derivable quantity
is supported within a stated retained population; this designation does not imply continuous acquisition
or applicability to every service offered by the protocol. A quantity requiring additional acquisition has a
plausible source or linkage requirement. Inapplicable quantities do not describe the sampled unit, whereas
unknown quantities lack sufﬁcient evidence to resolve their status. Figure S2 summarizes selected families;
the accompanying matrix includes the full deﬁnitions and qualiﬁcations.

D.3 Historical signing policies
Flare epoch artifacts identify policy signers, integer weights, a threshold and the starting voting round. These

Reward entitlement, signing weight and deposited capital have different units and roles. Epoch quantities

E Semantic rules and assistance disclosure
The correction ledger connects each changed ﬁeld to a native object, a historical rule and its previous interpre-
tation. UMA challenge corrections require an observed dispute; settlement corrections require a completed
lifecycle and the relevant token ﬂow. Winner payments separate posted principal from the unburned portion
29

of the losing bond. Tellor outcomes are interpreted from the challenge’s perspective: SUPPORT accepts
the challenge, while an AGAINST outcome does not justify an applied reporter loss merely because stake
entered escrow earlier.
VotingV2 requires a further distinction between request-level accrual and application to stake. A signed
accrual records an adjustment associated with a voting request. The later application event concerns the net
change actually applied to a stake position. The ledger retains the accrued amount and leaves a request-level
applied loss unknown when application evidence is not matched. Adding the accrual to a subsequent net
application would count stages of the same accounting process twice.
Historical binding is documented separately from a rule’s arithmetic. The retained register gives stronger
source-binding evidence for VotingV2 than for the OOv2 and Tellor implementations. A repository commit
identiﬁes inspected source code but does not alone establish which binary executed at every historical block.
Correction counts in Table 9 refer to the named populations and do not establish the validity of untested
ﬁelds.

The Polygon request, proposal and dispute are decoded from OOv2 events; Ethereum’s resolution is
decoded from VotingV2. The Polygon settlement retains the same resolved value. Native request hashing
and ancillary-data transformations support the identity match. The ﬁve retained transactions do not provide

and resolution is not attributed solely to voting or solely to transport.
The accounting record separates both posted deposits, the winner’s gross payout and the protocol fee. The
principal-return and gain components refer to the same payout and must not be counted again as separate
transfers. The conﬁgured reward is tracked separately from the observed payout components. No inde-
pendent factual adjudication is available for the underlying proposition, and the early-expiration resolution
retains its native meaning.
H Reconstruction and versioned evidence
Reproducibility is organized around the relationship between retained inputs, deterministic transformations
and named outputs. Input ﬁngerprints bind a calculation to the ﬁles it used. Source registries identify
the interfaces and historical boundaries, while result manifests connect tables and ﬁgures to the relevant
processing rules. A successful replay establishes consistency for those inputs and transformations.
The companion tutorial contains a bounded example for each of the eight branches. Each example veriﬁes
its retained input, reads native observation units and inspects the available clocks. These archived examples
are distinguished from fresh public-interface queries, whose results depend on archive retention and provider
behaviour. Access to one historical receipt or block does not demonstrate continuous acquisition across an
entire registered interval.
The supplementary reproduction record contains the executable commands, environment versions, valida-
tion output and unresolved acquisition requirements. Historical model experiments are preserved separately
for provenance; they are not evidence about the revised feature deﬁnitions. The product catalogue and
checksums identify the material accompanying this working-paper revision separately from earlier public
deposits.
